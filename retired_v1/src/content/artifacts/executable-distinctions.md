# Executable Distinctions: How Words Shape Power Literally and Computationally

**Document class:** Working long-form publication essay  
**Version:** v0.1  
**Promotion state:** Pending founder and research review

## They heard words. I saw an interpreter.

The phrase **just words** assumes that words are inert marks placed on top of a more real world of matter, action, and power.

That assumption fails anywhere a system is built to interpret symbols and propagate their consequences.

A programming language is made of words and symbols. Yet the system does not dismiss `class`, `private`, `implements`, `grant`, `deny`, `commit`, or `rollback` as rhetoric. Under a declared grammar and an executable interpreter, each term changes what may exist, who may act, which transitions are legal, and whether a prior state can be recovered.

The machine responds to a declaration by changing the reachable state of the system.

That is the central proposition:

> **Words become operators when they are recognized by a system authorized to act on their distinctions.**

The relevant system may be a compiler, database, operating system, court, bureaucracy, market, school, hospital, platform, or social institution. The mechanisms differ, but the general pathway remains recognizable:

```text
symbol
  -> interpreter or procedure
  -> recognized distinction
  -> classification
  -> authorized operation
  -> state transition
  -> consequence
  -> contestability and repair
```

Follow the complete rendered path, including the authority gate and contestability branch, in the [Original Visual Grammar](/language/visuals#symbol-to-consequence).

Words do not float above power. They often supply the address space through which power locates its object.

## Three layers of an executable distinction

The argument becomes misleading if token, meaning, and operation are collapsed.

### Token layer

The token is the mark, sound, label, or declared symbol presented to a system. The same token can mean different things under different grammars. At this layer, a word has not acquired one fixed meaning or material force.

### Semantic layer

At the semantic layer, a grammar, interpreter, or institution recognizes a distinction. The system assigns meaning, classifies a represented object, and makes some entities, states, relations, or obligations legible.

### Operational layer

At the operational layer, authority, rules, records, and execution paths determine what may happen because the distinction was accepted. An operation is permitted or prohibited, reachable state changes, and consequence is distributed.

The token does not act by itself. Consequence requires the coupling among representation, interpretation, authority, and an executable path.

## A class is not merely a label

The word **class** illustrates the point.

In ordinary institutional language, class may signify quality, education, social rank, or a group sharing a property. In software, a class is an explicit specification. It helps determine:

- what counts as an instance;
- what state an instance may contain;
- what behavior it supports;
- what relations it can form;
- what operations may be invoked;
- what invariants should remain true.

The declaration is symbolic, but its consequences are operational.

A class that omits a necessary state makes that state difficult or impossible to express. A class that conflates two distinct responsibilities forces every instance to carry the conflation. An access rule that marks an operation private removes it from other objects' admissible action sets. An interface that exposes the wrong abstraction distributes error across every dependent implementation.

The word does not merely describe the program. It helps construct the program's possibility space.

This is why the phrase **world class** can carry a serious formal turn.

A world class can be treated as a specification for a class of possible worlds: recognized entities, possible states, admissible operations, protected relations, failure modes, and repair paths.

The phrase is not a proof. It is a generative question:

> **What class of world does this system instantiate?**

## Institutional words are also connected to interpreters

Consider words such as **owner**, **citizen**, **employee**, **patient**, **dependent**, **authorized**, **eligible**, **approved**, **inadmissible**, **guilty**, and **terminated**.

None is merely descriptive once connected to an operative institution.

A recognized classification may determine who can access an account, enter a building, receive care, sign a document, inherit property, cross a border, speak in a proceeding, appeal a decision, or remain physically free.

The material force does not reside in the syllables alone. It resides in the coupling between:

1. a symbol;
2. an accepted grammar;
3. a recognized authority;
4. a system of records;
5. an execution path;
6. an enforcement boundary.

This is a computational structure in the broad sense: a represented state is evaluated under rules, an operation is selected, and a new state follows.

The result may be executed by silicon, by a clerk, by a judge, by an automated eligibility system, by an employer, or by a distributed institution. Human judgment may enter at several points. That does not make the process non-computational. It makes the interpreter hybrid, partial, and socially embedded.

## Naming determines visibility

The most consequential function of words may precede direct command.

Words determine what the system can represent.

Suppose an approval process recognizes only two values:

```text
approved
rejected
```

Reality may contain many more states:

- conditionally approved;
- approved by an unauthorized actor;
- approved but not funded;
- approved pending evidence;
- approved and later disputed;
- approved under coercion;
- approval expired;
- approval reversed;
- technically approved but institutionally unusable.

If the representation collapses these distinctions, the system may still appear clean. The omitted complexity has not vanished. It has been displaced into exception handling, spreadsheets, phone calls, appeals, human memory, inconsistent enforcement, or harm.

A defect is often a distinction the representation forgot but execution still needed.

This produces two governing principles:

> **What a system cannot name, it cannot reliably govern.**

> **What a system names incorrectly, it will act upon incorrectly.**

The political force of classification and the technical force of typing are not identical, but neither is merely verbal. Both influence which entities and states become legible to an operating system of rules.

## Software as an exposed laboratory

Software makes these mechanics unusually visible because execution demands explicitness.

A program eventually confronts questions that many institutions leave tacit:

- What kind of thing is this?
- Does it have an identity?
- What state is it in?
- What transitions are allowed?
- Who owns the operation?
- What may reference it?
- What creates it?
- What happens if creation fails halfway through?
- Can the state be restored?
- Who must be notified that the state changed?
- Which responsibility handles the request?

The familiar vocabulary of object-oriented software and design patterns records practical answers:

- Class specifies.
- Object embodies.
- Instance realizes.
- State situates.
- Method transforms.
- Interface governs contact.
- Strategy varies the route.
- Observer witnesses change.
- Mediator coordinates dependency.
- Memento preserves recovery state.
- Chain of Responsibility gives a request somewhere to land.
- Interpreter gives a grammar executable meaning.

These terms did not survive only because they sounded elegant. They survived because programmers repeatedly encountered recurring structural problems under operational pressure.

The language is therefore evidence—not final proof, but evidence—that software engineering has been handling fragments of a more general representational mechanics.

## Analogy or operational homology?

Vocabulary overlap is cheap.

The serious research question begins when the mapping preserves structure.

A weak analogy says that an institutional role is "kind of like" a software object.

A stronger correspondence asks whether both domains contain comparable:

- objects or roles;
- states;
- admissible transformations;
- interfaces;
- invariants;
- failure modes;
- repair operations;
- responsibility paths;
- composition rules.

When roles, relations, transformations, defects, and repairs correspond together, the mapping may be more than poetic resemblance. It may be an **operational homology**: two domains realizing a shared organizational pattern under different material carriers.

That claim must be tested, not merely admired.

The recurrence may arise because:

1. the domains share a deep formal structure;
2. one domain historically borrowed language from another;
3. humans reuse familiar words for different structures;
4. the mapping is useful locally but fails under composition;
5. the apparent unity is an artifact of selective attention.

A disciplined program must therefore search for broken mappings as actively as successful ones.

## Words, authority, and repair

A named distinction can distribute power, but naming alone does not legitimate the distribution.

Every executable classification should be accompanied by questions of authority and repair:

- Who defined the class?
- Who may classify an instance?
- What evidence is required?
- Which operations follow automatically?
- Who is affected but not represented?
- Can the classification be contested?
- Can it expire or be reversed?
- Does the system preserve the history required for appeal?
- Where does responsibility land if the classification is wrong?

This is where the software vocabulary becomes more than a metaphor for institutional design.

A robust system does not only define successful execution. It specifies invalid states, error propagation, logging, rollback, recovery, and ownership. Institutions often apply classifications with substantial consequences while leaving their equivalent repair semantics incomplete.

The problem is not that humans should be governed like software objects. The problem is that systems already act through representations while denying the need to make those representations observable, contestable, and repairable.

## The semantic layer is operational

Modern power is extensively mediated through code, contracts, statutes, credentials, forms, database schemas, account records, risk scores, diagnoses, titles, permissions, procedures, and models.

Each is a representational surface connected to action.

A person need not be physically barred if the word **ineligible** closes every relevant interface. A responsibility need not be openly refused if the system contains no class capable of owning it. A resource need not be destroyed if the word **invalid** makes it unreachable through every authorized operation.

The semantic layer is therefore not a decorative layer above real machinery.

> **The semantic layer is where much of the machinery is controlled.**

## From language to stewardship

Recognizing words as executable distinctions creates a burden of care.

The answer is not to freeze language or pretend perfect representation is possible. It is to design systems that can expose and revise their distinctions without abandoning coherence.

That requires:

- declared definitions;
- visible state models;
- explicit transition rules;
- preserved provenance;
- affected-party representation;
- appeal and repair paths;
- versioned schemas;
- witnessed changes;
- responsibility coupled to the ability to act.

The point is not that every word is a command.

The point is that every consequential system requires representation, and words are among the primary carriers through which representation becomes shareable, institutional, and executable.

They heard words.

The deeper object was the interpreter, the authority, the state machine, and the consequence path behind them.

> **There are no "just words" once words are wired to consequence.**

## Present claim ceiling and review state

This is a conceptual public argument supported by software and institutional examples. It does not establish that every word is executable, that human institutions are reducible to computers, or that recurring vocabulary across fields proves formal equivalence.
