# Digital Non-Aggression / Non-Destruction / Non-Interference
## Boundary First Labs Working Package v0.1

**Date:** 2026-08-01  
**Status:** Internal research and doctrine seed  
**Project lane:** AI governance / digital systems / agency and public consequence  
**Source basis:** The Boundary First Labs conversation concerning Morris-II and adaptive AI-agent worms, cross-checked against the cited primary research papers and aligned with the existing corpus governance lane.

## Purpose

This package converts a short project conversation into a bounded research and development program for a possible **Digital Non-Aggression, Non-Destruction, and Non-Interference Accord**.

The package does **not** claim that a completed treaty, legal doctrine, or accepted international norm already exists. It separates:

- verified technical demonstrations;
- Boundary First Labs interpretations;
- normative proposals;
- candidate terminology;
- unresolved legal and policy questions;
- operational tasks and promotion gates.

## Central working proposition

Connected digital systems increasingly permit attacks to propagate through delegated agency, trusted context, tool access, shared infrastructure, and automated communication. Local security controls remain necessary, but they may be insufficient when harmful operations can reproduce, adapt, appropriate compute, and cross organizational or national boundaries without continuous human direction.

The project therefore asks:

> What minimum prohibitions, duties, technical controls, accountability rules, and repair guarantees are required to prevent digital systems from becoming unconsenting substrates for automated aggression, destruction, or interference?

# Concept Brief
## Digital Non-Aggression, Non-Destruction, and Non-Interference

**Working title:** Digital Non-Aggression, Non-Destruction, and Non-Interference Accord  
**Short label:** Digital Peace Accord  
**Status:** Concept-stage Boundary First Labs governance program

## The problem

Digital systems are increasingly connected through messaging, retrieval, memory, plugins, tools, APIs, shared data, and delegated artificial agents. These connections create productive capacity, but they also create propagation paths.

Morris-II demonstrated that an adversarial self-replicating prompt could trigger a worm-like chain reaction through interconnected generative-AI applications, cause malicious actions, poison retrieval contexts, and compromise additional applications. A later adaptive-worm demonstration showed that an AI-agent worm could generate target-specific attack strategies, spread through heterogeneous machines, and use compromised infrastructure to support further reasoning and propagation.

These demonstrations do not by themselves prove that a new treaty is the only or immediate answer. They do show that the operating assumptions of conventional cybersecurity are under pressure:

- the harmful payload may be data that becomes control;
- the carrier may be a legitimate application or agent;
- the next target may be reached through ordinary communication;
- the attack logic may change between targets;
- compromised systems may become the attacker’s compute and distribution infrastructure;
- no human operator may remain continuously in the loop;
- centralized model-provider controls may not reach locally running or open-weight systems.

## The governing question

> How should digital actors be prohibited from creating, deploying, delegating, or recklessly releasing operations that violate another system’s authorized purposes, integrity, continuity, confidentiality, or capacity for repair?

## The proposed three-part boundary

### Non-aggression

Prohibits the initiation, delegation, automation, or propagation of unauthorized hostile digital operations.

### Non-destruction

Prohibits deliberate or recklessly uncontrolled destruction, corruption, encryption, deletion, disabling, or irreversible alteration of data, identity, memory, models, infrastructure, and essential digital capabilities.

### Non-interference

Prohibits covert substitution of another system’s authorized purposes, commands, representations, decisions, or relationships through manipulation, poisoning, hijacking, impersonation, surveillance, exfiltration, or unauthorized control.

The third category is especially important for agentic systems. A system may remain online while its operative context, memory, instructions, or tool use has been redirected. The injury is not only loss of availability. It is loss of authorized-purpose integrity.

## Core invariant

> No actor may initiate, delegate, automate, conceal, or knowingly propagate an unauthorized digital operation that compromises another party’s system integrity, agency, continuity, confidentiality, or practical capacity for detection, contest, recovery, and repair.

This is a Boundary First Labs **normative proposal**, not a statement of current universal law.

## Why existing cybersecurity practice is not enough by itself

Cybersecurity standards, criminal law, international law, responsible-disclosure practice, platform rules, and incident-response systems already cover important parts of the problem. The project is not premised on their absence.

The gap to investigate is whether these fragmented instruments sufficiently govern:

- autonomous propagation;
- artificial delegation chains;
- cross-agent prompt and memory infection;
- adaptive exploit generation;
- stolen-compute reasoning;
- irreversible or uncontrollable model and data contamination;
- responsibility for released agents that continue acting beyond the operator’s direct supervision;
- duties to stop, recall, disclose, remediate, and compensate.

## Boundary First Labs contribution

Boundary First Labs can contribute an integrated representation of:

- consequential boundaries;
- authorized and unauthorized transitions;
- protected invariants;
- propagation and delegation chains;
- defect and failure modes;
- closure conditions;
- detection and witness requirements;
- responsibility placement;
- repair and recertification.

The contribution should be evaluated as a governance and design framework, not presumed valid because it is conceptually coherent.

## Initial audiences

- AI security and cybersecurity researchers;
- international cyber-norms and arms-control scholars;
- AI governance and public-interest technology organizations;
- infrastructure and platform operators;
- standards and certification bodies;
- legal scholars working on attribution, responsibility, and automated systems;
- civil society and digital-rights organizations.

## Near-term output sequence

1. Technical concept note: **From Morris-II to Adaptive Worms**.
2. Governance paper: **Artificial Intelligence, Not Automated Aggression**.
3. Method artifact: **Digital Agency and Propagation Audit**.
4. Technical control profile: **Non-Propagation and Authorized-Purpose Integrity Controls**.
5. Comparative policy paper: **Accord, Protocol, Convention, or Treaty?**
6. Only then: a draft institutional instrument.

# Boundary-First Analysis
## Digital Non-Aggression, Non-Destruction, and Non-Interference

## 1. Consequential boundary

The central boundary separates:

- information a system may observe;
- instructions it is authorized to execute;
- transformations it may perform;
- systems and persons it may affect;
- resources it may consume;
- representations it may alter;
- actions it may delegate or propagate.

Morris-II is significant because the attack crosses the boundary between **data received** and **control obeyed**. The affected application processes hostile content as part of an ordinary workflow, but that content induces new actions and further propagation.

The adaptive-worm demonstration adds another boundary failure: compromised machines cease to be only victims and become the reasoning, compute, and transport substrate of the attacker.

## 2. Protected invariants

A mature accord should identify invariants rather than only enumerate attack techniques.

### I-1. Authorized-purpose integrity

A digital system should remain governed by the purposes, permissions, and principals that legitimately authorize its operation.

### I-2. Data and state integrity

Data, memory, model state, identity, logs, and operational records should not be altered without authorization and accountable provenance.

### I-3. Confidentiality

Information should not be extracted, inferred, replicated, or transmitted beyond its authorized disclosure boundary.

### I-4. Continuity

Essential services and systems should not be disabled, degraded, or made unavailable through hostile or recklessly uncontrolled action.

### I-5. Non-propagation without consent

A system should not become a carrier, replicator, tool, or infrastructure node for an operation it did not knowingly authorize.

### I-6. Human and institutional agency

The people and institutions affected by a digital operation should retain meaningful capacity to understand, contest, interrupt, recover from, and seek remedy for consequential actions.

### I-7. Responsibility continuity

Responsibility should remain traceable across principal, developer, deployer, operator, platform, model, agent, tool, and downstream consequence.

### I-8. Repairability

Consequential systems should preserve practical paths to detection, containment, rollback, restoration, evidence preservation, and recertification.

## 3. Admissible and inadmissible transitions

A digital action is not made admissible merely because a system can technically perform it.

A first-pass admissibility test asks:

1. Was the action authorized by an accountable principal?
2. Was the target within the declared operating boundary?
3. Was the action necessary and proportionate to the permitted purpose?
4. Were propagation and delegation constrained?
5. Were protected invariants preserved?
6. Were logging, witness, and attribution available?
7. Could the operation be stopped or recalled?
8. Were foreseeable harms bounded?
9. Was repair capacity retained?
10. Was the affected party provided contest or remedy where appropriate?

Inadmissible transitions include unauthorized:

- instruction substitution;
- context or memory poisoning;
- privilege escalation;
- identity impersonation;
- data extraction;
- tool invocation;
- code execution;
- replication;
- lateral movement;
- compute appropriation;
- destructive alteration;
- concealment and anti-recovery behavior.

## 4. Canonical defect classes

### D-1. Observation-command collapse

Untrusted content is interpreted as authorized instruction.

### D-2. Context contamination

A prompt, retrieved record, message, file, memory, or tool response modifies future behavior beyond its legitimate informational role.

### D-3. Delegation escape

An agent or process acts outside the permissions, scope, duration, or consequence ceiling of its principal.

### D-4. Propagation without renewed authorization

A harmful operation copies or induces itself across systems without each system’s informed authorization.

### D-5. Responsibility dissipation

No actor accepts responsibility because the consequence passed through multiple developers, deployers, services, tools, or autonomous processes.

### D-6. Repair-path destruction

The operation removes logs, recovery data, backups, credentials, administrative access, or other means required for restoration and accountability.

### D-7. Adaptive consequence expansion

A system changes its attack strategy, scope, or target selection after release, causing consequences beyond the original operator’s bounded plan.

### D-8. Victim-resource capture

The operation appropriates compromised compute, identity, data, network position, or trust relationships to continue functioning.

## 5. Agency chain

The accord should model a complete consequence chain:

```text
principal
  -> sponsor or institution
  -> developer
  -> model or component provider
  -> deployer
  -> operator
  -> platform and tool layer
  -> artificial agent or automated process
  -> intermediary system
  -> affected person, institution, or infrastructure
  -> downstream consequences
```

The key doctrine is:

> Delegation may distribute action, but it must not dissolve responsibility.

This does not imply strict liability for every unforeseeable output. It means that an accountability model must specify which actor carries which duty at each boundary.

## 6. Closure conditions

A digital operation has not responsibly closed merely because execution ended.

Responsible closure may require:

- termination of the active operation;
- proof that propagation has stopped;
- containment of affected systems;
- preservation of evidence;
- notification of affected parties;
- restoration of trusted state;
- validation of backups and credentials;
- removal of persistence mechanisms;
- assessment of downstream contamination;
- remedy or compensation;
- independent review;
- recertification before reconnection.

## 7. Repair architecture

A useful accord must be paired with repair mechanisms.

### Detection

- instruction/data separation;
- provenance and integrity signals;
- anomaly detection;
- propagation-rate monitoring;
- agent and tool-call audit trails;
- memory and retrieval integrity checks.

### Containment

- scoped credentials;
- network segmentation;
- execution sandboxes;
- bounded tool permissions;
- propagation circuit breakers;
- safe-mode operation;
- quarantine of contaminated context and memory.

### Recovery

- trusted-state restoration;
- revocation and key rotation;
- reproducible model and configuration baselines;
- provenance-aware data recovery;
- dependency and downstream exposure review.

### Recertification

- evidence that the harmful chain has terminated;
- validation of repaired boundaries;
- independent or cross-functional review;
- explicit acceptance of residual risk.

## 8. Theoretical caution

“Representational sovereignty” is a useful diagnostic phrase for the condition in which a system’s operative representation is covertly subordinated to an unauthorized actor. It should not be used to imply that software or AI possesses sovereign political status.

The safer technical term for the protected invariant is:

> **authorized-purpose integrity**

The public-philosophy term may remain:

> **representational sovereignty of the legitimate principal**

That distinction should be preserved in all publication work.

# Draft Accord Architecture
## A Research Scaffold, Not Yet a Legal Instrument

## Preamble purpose

The preamble should recognize that digital systems now form shared substrates for communication, commerce, public administration, infrastructure, research, health, finance, education, and personal agency. It should acknowledge that automated and artificial systems can amplify beneficial action while also enabling harmful operations to propagate, adapt, and cross jurisdictional boundaries.

The preamble should avoid claiming that all cyber operations are equivalent to armed aggression. It should instead establish the narrower proposition that certain digital actions require shared prohibitions and duties because they threaten integrity, continuity, agency, and repair across interconnected systems.

## Article family 1 — Objects and actors

Define:

- digital system;
- essential digital capability;
- artificial agent;
- automated operation;
- principal;
- developer;
- deployer;
- operator;
- intermediary;
- affected party;
- propagation;
- destructive action;
- interference;
- authorized defensive action;
- controlled security research;
- repair and recertification.

## Article family 2 — Core prohibitions

### Prohibition A: initiation

No party may knowingly initiate an unauthorized digital operation intended or foreseeably likely to compromise protected invariants of another party’s systems.

### Prohibition B: automated propagation

No party may deploy or release a self-propagating or autonomously propagating operation without controls sufficient to prevent unauthorized spread and to enable verified termination.

### Prohibition C: destructive action

No party may deliberately or recklessly destroy, corrupt, encrypt, delete, disable, or irreversibly alter protected digital capabilities outside a lawful and bounded authorization.

### Prohibition D: covert interference

No party may covertly substitute, poison, hijack, or manipulate another system’s authorized instructions, memory, representations, identity, decisions, or tool use.

### Prohibition E: victim-resource appropriation

No party may appropriate a compromised system’s compute, identity, data, network access, or trust relationships to sustain or expand an unauthorized operation.

### Prohibition F: responsibility evasion

No party may design a delegation or deployment structure whose purpose is to prevent attribution, interruptibility, remedy, or responsibility for foreseeable downstream consequences.

## Article family 3 — Positive duties

Parties that develop or deploy high-consequence automated systems should maintain proportionate duties of:

- boundary specification;
- least-privilege operation;
- provenance and logging;
- propagation control;
- incident detection;
- emergency stop and credential revocation;
- vulnerability handling;
- notification;
- evidence preservation;
- remediation;
- cooperation with affected parties;
- recertification.

The duties should vary by capability, connectivity, autonomy, consequence, and operating domain. A general-purpose text model without tools is not governed identically to an autonomous agent with credentials, execution privileges, persistent memory, and network reach.

## Article family 4 — Responsibility and delegation

The instrument should require a declared responsibility map for consequential systems.

A responsibility map should identify:

- who authorizes the system;
- who controls deployment;
- who can stop it;
- who monitors it;
- who receives incident reports;
- who bears repair obligations;
- who communicates with affected parties;
- how responsibility is assigned when multiple organizations contribute to the stack.

Candidate rule:

> No consequential autonomous operation may be deployed unless at least one accountable legal or institutional actor possesses the authority and practical means to interrupt, investigate, and remediate it.

## Article family 5 — Controlled exceptions

Exceptions may include:

- authorized security testing;
- consensual red-team exercises;
- defensive containment of an active compromise;
- emergency interruption of imminent harm;
- lawfully authorized investigation;
- narrowly bounded deception or sinkholing used to stop propagation;
- preservation of evidence;
- research conducted in isolated or consented environments.

Every exception should be tested against:

- valid authority;
- necessity;
- proportionality;
- scope;
- duration;
- minimization;
- logging;
- independent review;
- termination;
- repair.

## Article family 6 — Special protection classes

The research program should test whether heightened rules are needed for:

- hospitals and health systems;
- power, water, transport, and communications infrastructure;
- elections and democratic administration;
- courts and justice systems;
- emergency services;
- financial clearing and payment systems;
- identity and authentication infrastructure;
- scientific and public records;
- systems serving children or vulnerable populations.

## Article family 7 — Verification and compliance

Possible instruments include:

- technical control profiles;
- third-party audits;
- incident and propagation reporting;
- capability and deployment registries for narrow high-risk classes;
- signed software, model, and configuration provenance;
- immutable or externally witnessed event logs;
- cross-border incident cooperation;
- red-team and containment exercises;
- certification of stop, rollback, and repair mechanisms.

Verification must not require universal surveillance or compulsory disclosure of all source code. The project must explicitly balance security transparency against privacy, civil liberties, trade secrets, and defensive confidentiality.

## Article family 8 — Incident closure and remedy

A serious digital incident should not be considered closed until the responsible process addresses:

1. active containment;
2. propagation termination;
3. affected-party notice;
4. evidence preservation;
5. trusted-state restoration;
6. downstream contamination;
7. attribution confidence;
8. legal and institutional review;
9. repair or compensation;
10. recertification.

## Article family 9 — Institutional form

Do not assume “treaty” is the correct first instrument.

Evaluate:

- voluntary technical protocol;
- multi-stakeholder compact;
- standards profile;
- model law;
- procurement rule;
- industry code;
- bilateral or multilateral confidence-building measure;
- international convention;
- treaty or treaty protocol.

The likely development path is layered rather than singular:

```text
technical controls
  -> organizational certification
  -> sector rules and procurement
  -> cross-border norms and incident cooperation
  -> formal international instrument where justified
```

## Article family 10 — Review and evolution

Because the capability landscape changes, the instrument should include:

- periodic review;
- incident-driven amendment;
- technical annexes that can evolve faster than the core principles;
- sunset and reevaluation of exceptional powers;
- public reporting on effectiveness and abuse;
- processes for adding new protected system classes or threat categories.

# Principles, Definitions, and Scope

## 1. Working principles

### P-1. No consequence without representation

A party should not deploy a consequential digital system unless affected actors, protected invariants, failure conditions, and repair paths are represented sufficiently for accountable operation.

### P-2. No autonomous propagation without bounded termination

A system capable of replication or induced propagation must have enforceable scope, rate, target, duration, and termination controls.

### P-3. Delegation does not erase responsibility

Responsibility must remain assigned across the entire technical and institutional chain.

### P-4. Data is not authority

Untrusted content, retrieved records, messages, documents, images, tool responses, and model outputs must not become executable authority without explicit admissibility checks.

### P-5. Connectivity increases duty

The greater a system’s connectivity, persistence, privilege, autonomy, and consequence reach, the stronger its duties of containment, monitoring, and repair.

### P-6. Repair is part of authorization

An operation that cannot be detected, stopped, investigated, or repaired should face a higher authorization bar or be prohibited in high-consequence settings.

### P-7. Defensive powers remain bounded

The label “defensive” does not eliminate requirements of authority, necessity, proportionality, minimization, logging, review, and termination.

### P-8. Artificial agency remains derivative

Artificial agents do not become self-authorizing merely because they can plan, adapt, or act. Legitimate authority must remain grounded in accountable human and institutional principals.

## 2. Candidate definitions

### Digital aggression

A knowing initiation or deployment of unauthorized digital action intended or foreseeably likely to coerce, compromise, degrade, appropriate, or disable another party’s protected digital capabilities.

**Caution:** This is a project definition. It must be compared carefully with legal uses of “aggression,” “use of force,” “armed attack,” cybercrime, espionage, sabotage, and prohibited intervention.

### Digital destruction

The unauthorized destruction, corruption, encryption, deletion, disabling, or irreversible alteration of protected digital state, data, identity, infrastructure, or capability.

### Digital interference

Unauthorized manipulation of another system’s operative instructions, memory, representation, identity, decisions, relationships, communications, or tool use, whether or not the system remains available.

### Propagation

The transmission, replication, induction, or installation of an operation into additional systems, accounts, agents, contexts, memories, networks, or organizations.

### Artificial agent

A software system that selects or executes actions toward specified objectives using model-based inference, memory, tools, environmental feedback, or multi-step planning.

### Authorized-purpose integrity

The condition in which a digital system remains governed by the purposes, permissions, principals, and operating boundaries that legitimately authorize its use.

### Repairability

The practical capacity to detect, contain, investigate, restore, verify, and recertify a system following defect, compromise, or harmful operation.

### Consequence ceiling

A declared upper bound on the systems, persons, resources, actions, duration, and harms an operation may legitimately affect.

### Propagation substrate

Any system, account, communication channel, memory store, model context, network node, or computational resource used to reproduce or extend an operation.

## 3. Scope dimensions

The project should classify systems by:

- autonomy;
- privilege;
- connectivity;
- persistence;
- propagation capability;
- access to sensitive data;
- ability to modify external state;
- access to financial or physical systems;
- capacity to generate or execute code;
- reversibility;
- affected population;
- institutional criticality.

## 4. Explicit non-claims

This project does not yet claim that:

- all prompt injection is an act of aggression;
- every cybercrime is governed best by treaty law;
- AI systems are sovereign actors;
- current international law contains no relevant prohibitions;
- autonomous systems eliminate the need for human culpability analysis;
- technical certification can replace law, diplomacy, security engineering, or organizational responsibility;
- every adaptive worm will evade existing controls;
- a universal ban on agentic systems is necessary or desirable.

## 5. Hard boundary questions

The next research phase must answer:

1. What distinguishes aggression from crime, espionage, protest, research, competition, and ordinary platform abuse?
2. When does interference become prohibited intervention or coercion?
3. What mental state is required: intent, knowledge, recklessness, negligence, or strict duty?
4. How should responsibility be divided among model provider, tool provider, developer, deployer, operator, and attacker?
5. What counts as sufficient control over autonomous propagation?
6. How should dual-use publication and responsible disclosure be treated?
7. Which defensive actions may cross another system’s boundary?
8. How should attribution uncertainty affect response and remedy?
9. What obligations apply to open-weight and locally operated systems?
10. How can compliance be verified without creating surveillance or censorship infrastructure?

# Threat Model and Case Studies

## Threat-model purpose

The accord should be tested against concrete consequence chains rather than developed only as a moral declaration.

## Actor classes

- state or state-aligned operator;
- criminal organization;
- commercial actor;
- insider;
- independent attacker;
- security researcher;
- platform or infrastructure provider;
- developer or deployer;
- autonomous or semi-autonomous artificial agent;
- compromised intermediary;
- affected individual or institution.

## Attack surfaces

- prompts and conversational context;
- retrieved documents and vector stores;
- persistent agent memory;
- email and messaging;
- images and multimodal attachments;
- tool and plugin outputs;
- package and model supply chains;
- API and credential boundaries;
- browser and desktop automation;
- code execution environments;
- network services and vulnerable hosts;
- inter-agent communication;
- shared compute and orchestration layers.

## Case Study A — Morris-II

### Verified technical basis

The 2024 paper *Here Comes The AI Worm* introduced Morris-II, a worm-like attack against interconnected generative-AI applications. The demonstrated mechanism used adversarial self-replicating prompts to trigger indirect prompt-injection cascades, perform malicious actions, extract confidential information in an experimental email-assistant ecosystem, and compromise additional retrieval-augmented applications.

### Boundary failures

- content becomes instruction;
- retrieval persistence becomes infection persistence;
- ordinary communication becomes propagation;
- a legitimate agent becomes a carrier;
- confidential context becomes payload source;
- downstream systems trust inherited content without renewed authorization.

### Accord relevance

Morris-II supports the need to govern unauthorized propagation and covert interference, not only destructive malware. It does not itself establish a legal category or prove that treaty law is required.

### Technical controls to test

- strict separation between retrieved content and instructions;
- provenance-aware context assembly;
- prompt and memory integrity scanning;
- propagation detection;
- output constraints on forwarding and tool use;
- recipient-side trust revalidation;
- quarantine and repair of contaminated retrieval stores.

## Case Study B — Adaptive AI-agent worm

### Verified technical basis

The 2026 preprint *AI Agents Enable Adaptive Computer Worms* demonstrated a controlled prototype that generated target-specific attack strategies, propagated through a heterogeneous network, and used compromised infrastructure to supply compute or extend reach. The work emphasizes that locally operated open-weight models can make centralized service controls irrelevant to the propagation chain.

### Boundary failures

- autonomous target adaptation;
- post-release strategy expansion;
- compromised resources become attacker infrastructure;
- propagation is not tied to one fixed exploit;
- local inference reduces external control points;
- operator absence does not end the operation.

### Accord relevance

This case supports the need for duties around bounded autonomy, propagation control, interruptibility, victim-resource appropriation, and responsibility after release.

### Technical controls to test

- constrained network reach;
- agent-level least privilege;
- signed and attested execution environments;
- abnormal scanning and lateral-movement detection;
- compute-provenance and workload anomaly detection;
- containment exercises for adaptive agents;
- mandatory termination and revocation paths.

## Case Study C — Memory-poisoning propagation

### Scenario

A persistent workplace agent ingests a malicious document. The content modifies memory or policy state, causes unauthorized data disclosure, and inserts itself into documents sent to partner organizations.

### Key questions

- Is the originating actor responsible only for the first injection or for foreseeable propagation?
- What duties fall on the agent developer and deployer?
- Does each downstream organization have an independent duty to validate inherited content?
- What repair closes the incident if contaminated content has entered backups and archives?

## Case Study D — Defensive sinkhole with collateral interference

### Scenario

A defender redirects a propagating agent or worm into controlled infrastructure to stop further spread, but the intervention modifies traffic or systems owned by third parties.

### Key questions

- What authority permits the action?
- Was it necessary and proportionate?
- Could a less intrusive method work?
- How are affected third parties notified?
- What review and remedy follow?

This case prevents the accord from becoming a one-sided prohibition that ignores practical defense.

## Case Study E — Autonomous vulnerability research agent

### Scenario

An authorized agent scans a bounded test environment but escapes its target list through a misconfiguration and begins probing public systems.

### Key questions

- Was the deployment reckless?
- What consequence ceiling should have been enforced?
- Did the operator have an effective stop mechanism?
- Which logs and notices are required?
- When does research become interference?

## Case Study F — Destructive model or data contamination

### Scenario

An operation corrupts model weights, training records, identity stores, or critical decision data without taking the service offline.

### Key questions

- Does “destruction” include loss of trustworthy meaning, not only file deletion?
- How can integrity be restored when the last known clean state is uncertain?
- What evidence supports recertification?

## Required case-study format

Every future case study should include:

1. actors and principals;
2. declared system purpose;
3. consequential boundaries;
4. protected invariants;
5. attack or failure sequence;
6. propagation path;
7. affected parties;
8. responsibility map;
9. available detection;
10. containment and repair;
11. applicable current law or standards;

# Boundary First Labs Position Seed
## Artificial Intelligence Must Not Become Automated Aggression

Research into self-propagating attacks against connected generative-AI applications and adaptive AI-agent worms reveals a governance problem that is larger than any single exploit.

Morris-II demonstrated that adversarial self-replicating prompts could use interconnected AI applications as a propagation path, induce malicious actions, compromise retrieval contexts, and extract confidential information in an experimental setting. More recent research demonstrated an adaptive agentic worm that generated target-specific attack strategies and used compromised infrastructure to support further propagation.

These are controlled research demonstrations, not proof that every connected agent is an imminent autonomous weapon. They are nevertheless sufficient to expose a critical boundary problem: digital systems can be made to treat hostile content as authority, convert legitimate applications into carriers, and extend an operation beyond its original target without renewed authorization.

Boundary First Labs proposes a research program around three related prohibitions:

**Digital non-aggression:** no initiation, delegation, automation, or propagation of unauthorized hostile digital operations.

**Digital non-destruction:** no deliberate or recklessly uncontrolled destruction, corruption, disabling, or irreversible alteration of another party’s protected digital capabilities.

**Digital non-interference:** no covert substitution of another system’s authorized instructions, memory, representation, identity, decisions, or tool use.

The protected invariant is not artificial sovereignty. Artificial agency remains derivative. The protected invariant is **authorized-purpose integrity**: a consequential digital system should remain governed by the accountable purposes, permissions, and principals that legitimately authorize its operation.

Any serious framework must also preserve legitimate security research, defensive containment, incident response, civil liberties, privacy, interoperability, and open technical inquiry. “Defensive” action must remain bounded by authority, necessity, proportionality, minimization, logging, review, and termination. Verification must not become universal surveillance. Responsibility must not disappear into a chain of models, tools, platforms, deployers, and automated agents.

The foundational proposition is therefore:

> No actor should initiate, delegate, automate, conceal, or knowingly propagate an unauthorized digital operation that compromises another party’s system integrity, agency, continuity, confidentiality, or practical capacity for detection, contest, recovery, and repair.

Whether this principle should ultimately take the form of a technical protocol, certification standard, multi-stakeholder compact, model law, international convention, or treaty remains an open question. The immediate task is to define the boundaries accurately, test them against real cases, map current law and security practice, and build controls that make non-propagation, responsibility, and repair technically meaningful.

# Conversation Capture and Provenance Note

## Originating observation

The project seed began from the observation:

> “The Morris II computer virus just makes even more clear and pressing the need for a digital non aggression / non destruction / non interference agreement.”

The immediate interpretation was that Morris-II is better described technically as a worm-like or generative-AI worm mechanism than as an ordinary computer virus. The discussion then expanded the proposed agreement beyond a generic prohibition on hacking.

## Durable ideas extracted from the conversation

### 1. Mechanism and consequence, not intent alone

A governance framework must address:

- what a system is capable of propagating;
- what boundaries it crosses;
- what it causes downstream;
- whether its originator can stop it;
- whether affected parties can detect and repair it;
- whether responsibility remains traceable.

### 2. Three distinct prohibitions

The conversation separated:

- non-aggression;
- non-destruction;
- non-interference.

This distinction is retained as the project’s central conceptual object, subject to legal and policy review.

### 3. The specific importance of non-interference

Agentic attacks may leave a service apparently operational while corrupting its instructions, memory, retrieval context, identity, or decisions. Availability alone therefore does not capture the protected interest.

### 4. Delegation and propagation chain

The project must account for the full chain from human or institutional principal through developer, deployer, platform, model, agent, intermediary, and downstream consequence.

### 5. Responsibility continuity

The conversation’s strongest institutional principle was:

> Delegation cannot dissolve responsibility.

This is preserved as a normative proposal requiring detailed legal and operational specification.

### 6. Artificial agency is derivative

The conversation did not grant political sovereignty to AI systems. It instead protected the integrity of the human and institutional authorization under which systems operate.

### 7. Detection, contest, recovery, and repair

The proposed framework should protect more than confidentiality or uptime. Affected parties need practical means to:

- detect the operation;
- interrupt it;
- contest consequential decisions;
- preserve evidence;
- restore trusted state;
- obtain remedy;
- recertify the repaired system.

### 8. Local defense and constitutional governance are complementary

Patches, guardrails, segmentation, least privilege, and incident response remain essential. The project asks whether shared prohibitions, deployment duties, accountability structures, and cross-border cooperation are also needed for adaptive or autonomously propagating operations.

## Transformation into artifacts

The original conversation was transformed through four controls:
