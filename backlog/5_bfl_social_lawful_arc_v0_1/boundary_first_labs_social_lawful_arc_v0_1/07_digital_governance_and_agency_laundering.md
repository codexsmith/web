# Digital Governance and Agency Laundering

## The internet’s actual social role

The internet is often described as a communication platform. That description is accurate but radically incomplete because communication is the substrate of coordination, transaction, identity, knowledge, culture, politics, and collective action.

Digital systems do not merely transmit speech. They:

- rank and amplify it;
- infer from it;
- monetize attention to it;
- construct audiences;
- govern identity and access;
- mediate transactions;
- alter which social states become probable.

The internet introduced a radically new communication topology without an equivalent public protocol layer for accountability and repair. Private platforms filled the gap.

## Definition: agency laundering

> **Agency laundering is the distribution of an initiating actor’s intention, incentive, authority, or causal pressure across intermediary agents so that the desired consequence is preserved while responsibility becomes obscured, fragmented, or denied.**

A digital chain may look like:

\[
A_0 \rightarrow P \rightarrow G \rightarrow U_1,\ldots,U_n \rightarrow C,
\]

where:

- \(A_0\) supplies the goal or incentive;
- \(P\) supplies the platform;
- \(G\) supplies ranking, targeting, or generation;
- users or contractors supply locally voluntary actions;
- \(C\) is the composed consequence.

Each participant can claim to have performed only one permissible local step. The system-level consequence remains real.

## Compositional defect

The central formal candidate is:

> **Locally admissible actions can compose into a globally inadmissible system.**

This is especially likely when:

- no agent observes the full chain;
- responsibility is bounded locally while consequence propagates globally;
- the objective function is hidden or privately chosen;
- incentives are distributed through ranking, payment, or recommendation;
- speed prevents review before consequence;
- automation multiplies reach;
- affected parties lack standing inside the system.

## Accountability reconstruction

A digital governance audit should ask:

1. Who selected the objective?
2. Who supplied money or incentive?
3. Who designed the routing?
4. Who possessed predictive knowledge?
5. Who controlled amplification?
6. Who benefited?
7. Who could interrupt the chain?
8. Which agents were represented as independent but were coordinated?
9. Where did responsibility become weaker than control?
10. What repair is available to affected people?

## Regulatory object

The regulatory object should not be only individual content. It should include the architecture of mediated coordination:

- objective functions;
- amplification and recommendation;
- sponsorship and provenance;
- data collection and inference;
- automation and synthetic participation;
- identity and affiliation representation;
- interoperability and exit;
- due process and appeal;
- liability and repair;
- public representation in high-consequence systems.

## Core principles

- **Delegated agency does not erase originating agency.**
- **An intermediary’s agency does not cleanse the principal’s foreseeable causal contribution.**
- **Agency may be distributed; responsibility must remain reconstructable.**
- **No privately chosen objective should invisibly govern civilization-scale communication.**
- **The platform is an agent and remains inside the audit.**
