# Economic Fabric and Concentration Mechanics

## Economy as a distributed state-transition system

The economy is not only a set of balances. It is a distributed system in which money, credit, ownership, law, infrastructure, and social trust determine which transitions are reachable by which agents.

For an agent in state \(s\), resources modify the reachable action set:

\[
\mathcal{R}(s\mid m, c, a, i, t)
\]

where \(m\) is money, \(c\) is credit, \(a\) is access, \(i\) is institutional standing, and \(t\) is available time.

Money can therefore be described as:

> **a transport medium for economic possibility.**

It enables or forecloses transitions such as shelter, food, movement, medical care, education, legal defense, recovery from interruption, and future planning.

## Center and edge

The center and edge do not use money in the same way.

The center can often:

- store claims;
- wait through volatility;
- buy optionality;
- leverage assets;
- diversify risk;
- convert scale into lower transaction cost.

The edge must often:

- convert income immediately into survival;
- maintain household and local business circuits;
- absorb price shocks without reserves;
- borrow against future labor;
- accept high-cost terms because exit is unavailable.

The edge is not economically unimportant because it lacks stored capital. It is where the system’s human function becomes concrete.

> A distributed network with powerful servers and failing edge devices is not healthy merely because aggregate compute is high.

## Concentration field effect

Concentrated purchasing power changes the environment around it. It alters:

- bids for land and housing;
- expected product margins;
- service models;
- educational and credential baselines;
- insurance and healthcare structures;
- political access;
- the opportunity cost of serving low-resource users;
- the prerequisites for ordinary participation.

The minimum operating floor rises even when lower-income agents receive no additional capacity.

This is second-order inequality:

\[
\text{resource concentration}
\rightarrow
\text{market reorientation}
\rightarrow
\text{higher participation floor}
\rightarrow
\text{edge incapacity}
\rightarrow
\text{greater dependency}
\rightarrow
\text{further concentration}.
\]

## Weighted demand

Need does not directly determine production. Effective purchasing power weights the signal.

A conceptual expression is:

\[
D_i = N_i \cdot P_i,
\]

where \(N_i\) is need or desire and \(P_i\) is purchasing power. A severe need with weak purchasing power can produce a smaller market signal than a minor preference backed by concentrated wealth.

This is not a complete economic model. It is a representational warning:

> The market can read purchasing capacity as importance.

## Two superimposed economies

The conversation distinguishes:

1. **Operational economy** — money circulates through labor, maintenance, goods, services, infrastructure, and household reproduction.
2. **Claims economy** — ownership, debt, securities, derivatives, and valuations are exchanged and recursively repriced.

The two are connected but not identical. Financial claims may appreciate while the represented productive or social system deteriorates.

> The representation of economic value can improve while the represented economy loses agency.

## Economic success condition

A declared Boundary-First normative axiom is:

> An economic order should preserve sufficient operational capacity for every person to reproduce the conditions of continued life, participation, and meaningful agency.

This is a moral and political commitment, not a theorem derived from mechanics alone. Once declared, however, it gives the economic system a testable lower-bound requirement.
