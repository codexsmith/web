# Bounded AI

**Subtitle:** Forge what helps. Certify what acts. Forbid what dominates.  
**Version:** v0.2 public projection  
**Artifact type:** public AI-governance doctrine / synthesis essay  
**Audience:** general / policy / technical  
**Publication stage:** review  
**Claim maturity:** governance-doctrine-review  

## Summary

AI governance should not impose the same burden on every use, and it should not treat every powerful deployment as an ordinary product decision.

Boundary First Labs proposes a three-region public map:

- **Forge** — bounded assistance that expands human capacity while responsibility remains traceable.
- **Certificate** — stronger declared authority, evidence, contestability, accountability, monitoring, remedy, and revocation when AI-enabled systems act consequentially on others.
- **Prohibition** — uses or capabilities that should be prohibited or exceptionally restricted rather than normalized as merely higher-risk deployments.

This is a governance doctrine under review, not a completed regulatory framework, recognized certification program, legal instrument, or claim of regulatory authority.

## Keep different governance questions distinct

AI policy becomes harder to reason about when several different questions are collapsed into one category called capability or safety.

A useful working separation is:

```text
Capability != Agency != Authority != Accountability
```

- **Capability:** what can the system do?
- **Agency:** what can the deployed system cause to happen?
- **Authority:** what is it permitted to cause, by whom, where, and under what conditions?
- **Accountability:** who answers when the consequence is wrong or the boundary fails?

These terms are related but not interchangeable.

A system can possess a capability without having access to exercise it. It can possess operational access without legitimate delegated authority. It can possess delegated authority while accountability remains diffuse. A comparatively modest model can also create severe consequence when embedded in a high-authority institutional workflow.

This decomposition is offered as an analytical representation under review, not as a claim that Boundary First Labs invented these distinctions or that existing governance frameworks lack equivalent concepts.

## The governance object is larger than the model

A model never acts in isolation. It acts through tools, products, permissions, data, persistence, infrastructure, operators, incentives, interfaces, and institutional authority.

The same model can therefore occupy very different governance positions. A private drafting assistant and an automated system that materially changes another person's institutional options are not the same governance object simply because both use generative AI.

The question is not only what the model can do. It is what agency the deployed system can exercise, who authorized it, who bears the consequence, and whether an affected person can inspect, contest, reverse, or repair the result.

## Region 1 — Forge

Encourage useful AI where it helps people think, make, learn, research, translate, create, inspect, or repair without silently acquiring authority over other people.

**Boundary:** assistance does not itself create authority, and automation does not make responsibility disappear.

## Region 2 — Certificate

When an AI-enabled system can materially act on others, require a stronger operating boundary.

The word **certificate** is not intended to mean a badge, trust seal, or claim that a system has been proven safe.

A candidate Boundary First certificate is better understood as a **bounded, inspectable, revocable declaration of delegated authority**. It asks:

1. Who authorized the system?
2. For what purpose and in what domain?
3. Who can be affected?
4. What may it do, and what may it not do?
5. What must remain invariant?
6. What evidence supports deployment?
7. How is operation monitored?
8. How can an affected person contest the result?
9. Who owns remedy and accountable consequence?
10. When must authority be suspended, revised, or revoked?

In substance, the declaration says:

> This system may exercise these actions, in this domain, for this purpose, under this authority, affecting these parties, while preserving these invariants, supported by this evidence, under these monitoring and contest conditions, until these suspension or revocation conditions are met.

**Boundary:** a certificate is a scoped governance claim, not a guarantee of safety, legality, fairness, conformity, or permanent validity.

Existing assurance, authorization, conformity-assessment, impact-assessment, licensing, incident-reporting, or regulatory mechanisms may already carry some or all of these functions. The research question is whether this representation helps make delegated authority easier to inspect.

## Region 3 — Prohibition

Some uses should not become ordinary deployments simply because more monitoring or paperwork can be added.

A prohibition boundary should remain visible where the deployment itself would defeat meaningful human control, lawful accountability, contestability, or other non-negotiable public constraints.

**Boundary:** disputed prohibitions remain questions for law, democratic governance, evidence, rights, due process, and domain-specific review—not automatic conclusions produced by BFL terminology.

## Make control testable

A statement such as “humans remain in control” is too weak unless the claimed control can be witnessed.

A useful control claim should identify:

```text
authorization
-> observability
-> interruption
-> override / constraint
-> suspension
-> revocation
-> responsibility
-> repair
```

Not every system requires every mechanism in the same form. The burden should be proportional to exercised agency and consequence. But a claimed control mechanism should be identifiable and testable.

The purpose is not to multiply paperwork. It is to turn control from an aspiration into an operating claim that can be criticized and falsified.

## Capability removal needs precise language

A public interface refusing a behavior is not necessarily evidence that the underlying capability is gone.

Governance should avoid silently collapsing:

- **hidden capability** — not visible through the ordinary current interface;
- **suppressed capability** — discouraged or blocked by a control layer;
- **inaccessible capability** — not reachable under current interfaces or permissions;
- **revoked authority** — an otherwise reachable action is no longer permitted;
- **removed capability** — evidence supports that the relevant capability or transition is no longer operationally reachable under the declared threat and deployment model.

These are different claims and may require different evidence.

## Representation should mean the consequence-bearing representation

An institution can possess extensive information about a person while the actual decision path operates on a much smaller representation.

For governance purposes, the critical question is often:

> **What representation is actually executable in the decision path?**

A large case file may exist upstream while a downstream action is triggered by something like:

```text
risk_score = 0.81
eligible = false
fraud_flag = true
```

A useful review should distinguish the condition in the world, the information available, the representation admitted into the process, the executable representation that actually drives action, the authorized transition, the consequence, and the contest and repair path.

## Existing-governance context

Boundary First is not starting from an empty field. Existing AI-risk, assurance, impact-assessment, conformity-assessment, oversight, lifecycle-monitoring, and prohibited-use frameworks already provide substantial machinery.

The narrower research question is whether the Forge → Certificate → Prohibition representation, together with the capability / agency / authority / accountability separation, makes the authority and consequence boundary easier to inspect without duplicating, obscuring, or weakening established practice.

## Related diagnostic

The related **No Consequence Without Representation** diagnostic asks who acts, who is affected, which representation actually drives the decision, what distinctions must survive, whether the process can be meaningfully contested, where responsibility lands, and whether the consequence can actually be repaired.

The diagnostic does not certify legality or safety by itself.

## Claim firewall

This review draft does not claim:

- a completed AI regulatory framework;
- a new enforceable treaty;
- that a new international treaty is necessarily required;
- that certification proves safety, legality, fairness, conformity, or permanent validity;
- that all AI deployments require certification;
- that all automation is aggression;
- that Boundary First invented capability/agency/authority/accountability distinctions, AI risk management, assurance, impact assessment, conformity assessment, oversight, monitoring, or prohibited-use governance;
- that the proposed certificate schema is complete or empirically superior; or
- that governance language replaces technical safety, privacy, security, robustness, law, or domain-specific controls.

## Review request

The current publication should be tested against established AI-governance practice and real deployment cases. Useful criticism includes:

- where the representation duplicates existing frameworks;
- where its categories are technically or legally unusable;
- where existing policy vocabulary is better than BFL's terms;
- where it hides important domain-specific distinctions;
- which control claims can actually be operationalized; and
- what evidence would be required before stronger claims could be promoted.
